The Millbrook Tramways: A Short History

The first trams in Millbrook were horse-drawn. In 1879 a private company, the Millbrook and District Tramway Company, laid a single line of standard-gauge track from the railway station along the High Street to the market square, a distance of a little under two miles. Two horses pulled each car, and a third was added at the foot of Castle Hill, where the gradient reached one in fourteen. The fare was twopence for the full journey and a penny for any part of it. Within five years the company was carrying nearly a million passengers a year, but it never paid a dividend: horses were expensive to feed and stable, and the company's stables on Tanner's Lane burned down twice, in 1884 and again in 1891.

In 1898 the town council bought the company under the Tramways Act and resolved to electrify the system. The council's engineer, a man named Edwin Harrow who had worked on the electric lines in Leeds, recommended overhead wires fed at 550 volts direct current from a new generating station beside the gasworks. Electrification was completed in the spring of 1902. The council ordered twenty-four double-deck cars with open tops from a builder in Preston, and extended the network to four routes: the original station line, a northern route to the cemetery and the new housing at Hill Top, an eastern route along the canal to the ironworks, and a southern route through Ashford Green to the park gates. By 1910 the network had grown to just over eleven route miles.

The years before the First World War were the high point of the system. The trams ran from five in the morning until after eleven at night, with workmen's cars at half fare before seven. On Saturdays in summer, extra cars carried crowds to the park, where a bandstand and a boating lake had been laid out by the council partly to give the tramways something to carry. Covered top decks were fitted to most of the fleet between 1912 and 1914, after a series of letters to the local newspaper complaining about passengers arriving at work soaked by the rain.

The war brought shortages of staff and materials. Many of the conductors and some of the drivers joined the army, and from 1916 the council employed women as conductors for the first time; by 1918 more than half of the conductors were women. Maintenance was cut back and the track, much of it laid in 1901, began to wear badly. When the war ended the women conductors were dismissed to make room for returning servicemen, a decision that caused a public meeting of protest in the Corn Exchange but was not reversed.

In the 1920s the council faced a choice. The track needed renewal at a cost the engineer put at forty thousand pounds, and private motor buses had begun to compete on the busiest routes. The council renewed the station and southern lines in 1924 and 1925, and bought eight new enclosed cars with upholstered seats, but it decided against extending the system to the new estates on the western side of the town. Instead, in 1928 it started its own motor bus service to the western estates, the first time the corporation had run buses.

The eastern route along the canal was the first to close. Traffic to the ironworks had fallen when the works went over to short-time working in 1930, and the track on the canal road was in the worst condition on the system. The last tram ran on the eastern route on the thirty-first of March 1931, and the route was taken over by buses the next morning. The northern route followed in 1934, after the council concluded that buses could climb the hill to Hill Top more cheaply than trams, whose motors overheated on the long gradient in summer.

Trolleybuses were considered as a replacement for the remaining routes, since they could use the existing overhead power supply. A trial trolleybus ran on the southern route for three weeks in 1935 and was popular with passengers, who liked its quiet running. But the council had just signed a long contract with a bus manufacturer, and the general manager of the transport department was a firm believer in the motor bus. The trolleybus proposal was dropped in 1936.

The Second World War delayed the final closure. Fuel for buses was rationed, and the tramways, which ran on electricity from the corporation's own station, were suddenly valuable. Cars that had been stored at the Ashford Green depot were brought back into service, and the station and southern routes carried record numbers of passengers between 1941 and 1945, many of them workers at the munitions factory that had taken over the old ironworks site. Once again women were employed as conductors, and this time several also qualified as drivers.

After the war the track and the cars were worn out, and the council voted in 1947 to replace the last two routes with buses. The southern route closed in the summer of 1949. The final tram, car number 17, ran from the station to the market square on the evening of the twenty-fourth of September 1949, decorated with flags and carrying the mayor, the general manager and a party of retired staff, among them one of the drivers who had driven horse trams in the 1890s. Several thousand people lined the High Street to watch.

Little of the system survives. Most of the track was lifted for scrap in 1950 and 1951, although a short length can still be seen in the cobbles outside the old Ashford Green depot, which is now a furniture warehouse. Car number 17 was sold to a farmer and used as a henhouse for thirty years before it was rescued by a preservation society in 1982. After a restoration that took fourteen years, it now runs on summer weekends at a transport museum about forty miles away. The original generating station beside the gasworks was demolished in 1968, but the council's tramway offices on Bridge Street, with the corporation coat of arms above the door, are now a listed building.

Historians of the system have drawn two main conclusions. The first is that the council's decision not to extend the trams to the western estates in the 1920s, made to save money in the short term, left the system without any growth in traffic just as buses began to compete. The second is that the tramways shaped the town more than any other public work of the period: the housing at Hill Top and Ashford Green was built along the tram routes, and the town's main shopping streets are still the streets the trams ran along.
