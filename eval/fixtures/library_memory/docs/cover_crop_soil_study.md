# Winter Cover Crops and Soil Microbial Communities in a Clay-Loam Rotation: A Three-Season Field Trial

## Abstract

Winter cover crops are promoted to improve soil health, but their effect on soil microbial communities in heavy soils is poorly documented. We compared a cereal rye and hairy vetch mixture with bare fallow over three winters in a wheat–barley–oilseed rotation on clay loam. Cover-cropped plots had 12 % higher bacterial Shannon diversity and 18 % higher potentially mineralisable nitrogen by the third season. Earthworm counts doubled. Grain yields did not differ significantly between treatments in any year. The benefits accumulated slowly: no microbial difference was detectable after the first winter.

## 1. Introduction

Soil microbial communities drive nutrient cycling, aggregate formation and the breakdown of crop residues. Cover crops add root exudates and residues during the months when fields would otherwise lie bare, and so may feed microbial growth. Most published trials are on sandy or silty soils in warmer climates. Clay loams drain slowly, warm late in spring and are harder to establish cover crops on, so results may not transfer.

We asked three questions: whether a rye–vetch cover changes microbial diversity and composition; whether it changes nitrogen supply to the following cash crop; and whether it affects yield.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Site and design

The trial ran from autumn 2022 to summer 2025 at a research farm on a clay-loam soil (32 % clay, pH 6.8, 3.1 % organic matter). We used a randomised complete block design with four blocks and two treatments, giving eight plots of 24 m × 12 m.

### 2.2 Treatments

The cover treatment was sown in mid-September each year at 60 kg/ha cereal rye and 25 kg/ha hairy vetch, drilled directly after the cash-crop harvest. It was terminated with a roller-crimper in early April, three to four weeks before drilling the spring crop. Fallow plots were kept weed-free with one glyphosate application in October. All plots received the same fertiliser: 120 kg N/ha for wheat and 90 kg N/ha for barley and oilseed rape.

### 2.3 Sampling and analysis

We took soil cores (0–15 cm) from each plot in late April, two weeks after termination. Microbial DNA was extracted and the bacterial 16S rRNA gene (V4 region) and fungal ITS2 region were sequenced. Potentially mineralisable nitrogen was measured by a 7-day anaerobic incubation. Earthworms were counted by hand-sorting four 25 cm × 25 cm pits per plot in October. Yields were measured with a plot combine.

### 2.4 Statistics

Treatments were compared with linear mixed models with block as a random effect, separately for each season. Community composition was compared with PERMANOVA on Bray–Curtis distances.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Microbial diversity and composition

After the first winter, bacterial and fungal diversity did not differ between treatments. By the third season, bacterial Shannon diversity was 12 % higher under cover crops (p = 0.02); fungal diversity did not change significantly. Bacterial composition separated by treatment in seasons two and three (PERMANOVA R² = 0.21, p = 0.01), with more Acidobacteria and Verrucomicrobia under cover. Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi increased in relative abundance by about a third.

### 3.2 Nitrogen

Potentially mineralisable nitrogen was 18 % higher in cover-cropped plots in season three (p = 0.03), with a smaller, non-significant increase in season two. Soil nitrate at spring drilling was slightly lower under cover, consistent with uptake by the vetch–rye mixture.

### 3.3 Earthworms and yield

Earthworm counts doubled in cover-cropped plots by the third autumn (from a mean of 110 to 225 per m²). Grain yields did not differ significantly in any year. In the second year, barley after cover yielded 0.3 t/ha less than after fallow, which we attribute to slow drying and late drilling on the crimped residue.

## 4. Discussion

The slow build-up of microbial effects matches trials on other soils, where differences emerged only after several seasons. The larger nitrogen pool suggests that, over time, fertiliser rates after cover crops could be reduced, but we did not test reduced rates. The absence of a yield gain is common in short trials; the costs of seed and establishment were not recovered in three years.

## 5. Limitations

This is a single-site trial on one soil type, with four replicates, so statistical power is limited. We sampled only the topsoil and only in spring. We measured potential, not actual, nitrogen supply. Weather in 2023 was unusually wet, which delayed termination by two weeks.

## 6. Conclusions

On clay loam, a rye–vetch winter cover raised bacterial diversity, mineralisable nitrogen and earthworm numbers within three seasons, without changing yields. Farmers should expect soil benefits to take several years and plan termination early enough to avoid late drilling.
